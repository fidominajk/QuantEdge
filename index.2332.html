Mental maths tricks

These techniques come from Secrets of Mental Math by Arthur Benjamin and Michael Shermer. This is just a taster - the book goes much deeper and is genuinely worth your time.

Multiply any two-digit number by 11

Add the two digits together and place the result between them. If the middle sum is 10 or more, carry 1 into the left digit. Once you see it, you can never unsee it.

32 × 11 - place 3+2=5 in the middle - 352
47 × 11 - 4+7=11, carry the 1 - 517
Squaring any number ending in 5

Take the tens digit, multiply it by the next number up, then attach 25. The answer always ends in 25 - no exceptions.

35² - 3×4=12, attach 25 - 1,225
75² - 7×8=56, attach 25 - 5,625
Add left to right, not right to left

School taught you to start from the units. In your head, start from the left - biggest part first, then refine. You'll hold a running total much more easily this way.

58 + 36 - 50+30=80, then 8+6=14 - 94
147 + 85 - 100+80=180, then 47+5=52 - 232
Subtract by asking what's missing

Instead of borrowing digits, ask what you need to add to reach the next round number. Turns subtraction into addition, which the brain handles more naturally.

100 - 63 - what does 63 need to reach 100? - 37
200 - 147 - 147 needs 53 to reach 200 - 53
Multiply by rounding and adjusting

When a number is close to a round figure, multiply by the round number and correct for the difference. Much faster than working with the original.

29 × 7 - treat as 30×7=210, subtract 7 - 203
49 × 6 - 50×6=300, subtract 6 - 294
The criss-cross method

Break each number into tens and units, multiply the parts separately, and add. Work left to right - tens × tens first, cross products next, then units × units.

42 × 37 - 40×30=1200, then 40×7+30×2=340, then 2×7=14 - 1,554
Mental division - left to right

Divide from left to right. Estimate how many times the divisor goes into the leading digits, note the remainder, carry it forward. The most natural way the brain handles division.

143 ÷ 7 - 7 into 14 is 2, remainder 0; 7 into 3 - remainder 3 - answer 20 r3
Guesstimation

Round each number to its leading digit before operating. Gets you a ballpark in seconds - useful for checking whether your exact answer is even in the right range.

4,867 × 32 - round to 5,000×30=150,000. Exact: 155,744. Within 4%.
The percentage flip

X% of Y is always the same as Y% of X. Pick whichever direction is easier. This one surprises most people the first time they see it.

4% of 75 - hard. Flip: 75% of 4 = 3
8% of 25 - flip: 25% of 8 = 2
Squaring any two-digit number

Find the nearest round number, use the identity n² = (n+d)(n-d) + d² to make the multiplication easy. Choose d to land on a round number.

43² - nearest round is 40, d=3. So 46×40+9 = 1,840+9 = 1,849

Tips for quant tests

Strategic approach

Simple techniques that make a real difference. Worth five minutes before your first test.

The noise principle

Every question contains details that are there to distract you. Not accidentally – deliberately. They test whether you can separate what matters from what doesn't. The moment you spot noise, mentally cross it out. Don't try to incorporate it. Don't wonder if it becomes relevant later. It doesn't.

Example

"A delivery driver travels from a depot to Client A – 120 km away – at 80 km/h, then continues to Client B, a further 180 km, at 90 km/h. His van has a payload capacity of 800 kg and a 55-litre fuel tank. He stops briefly to update his route log. What is his average driving speed for the full journey?"

The payload, the fuel tank, the route log – none of it touches the calculation. Cross it out and move on.

Another example

"A bakery has been trading for 14 years and sources its flour from a local mill 8 miles away. Last month they sold 480 loaves at £2.80 each with a 28% margin. What was the total profit from loaves?"

14 years. 8 miles. Gone. You need 480, £2.80, and 28%. That's it.

The more intimidating a question looks, the more noise it usually contains. Long questions are often easier than short ones once you strip them back.

Read the last line first

Know exactly what you're solving for – the actual number, the unit, the format – before you read a single piece of context. This completely changes how you absorb the rest of the question. You'll read the preamble with a filter already in place, and the noise becomes much easier to spot.

Without this habit, you read a paragraph of context, form a vague impression of what it's about, then reach the question and have to re-read everything. Under time pressure, that's expensive.

Know when to skip – that's a skill too

If you've read a question twice and still don't see a clear path, move on. A question you can't crack in 30 seconds is costing you a question you could answer in 20.

Unanswered questions and wrong answers score the same – so if time is running short, a considered guess beats leaving it blank. The candidates who score highest aren't always the ones who solve every question. They're the ones who manage the clock.

Check the format (not just the number)

A significant number of marks are lost not because the calculation is wrong, but because the answer is in the wrong form. Read the format instruction carefully, every single time.

"Give your answer in £k, to 2 decimal places" – not £m, not pence, not rounded to a whole number.
"State increase or decrease" – a number alone won't do it.
"Give your answer as a fraction in its simplest form" – 4/8 is wrong. 1/2 is right.

This platform marks format. So does the real assessment.

The 5-second sanity check

After you answer, ask: does this make sense in the real world? A profit margin of 340%? A car travelling at 3 km/h? A company with 0.6 employees? These are signals worth pausing on. Five seconds of common sense is often all it takes to catch an error before it's submitted.

On being thrown by a question

It will happen. A question might land that feels unfamiliar or more complex than expected. That's part of the design.

The natural instinct is to slow down and try to force it. Often the better move is to strip it back: what is it actually asking? What numbers do you have? What connects them? Most questions that feel hard are familiar problems in unfamiliar framing.

If you genuinely can't find the path – skip it, move on, come back. One question is never the difference.
